Myosin and kinesin are the motors that move material inside living cells. Extracted from cells, they are attractive for devices such as biosensors, because they are efficient and nanoscale in size. In these devices the motors are fixed to a surface and push filaments, which act as molecular shuttles that carry the analyte.

The trouble is that some motors are damaged when they stick to the surface. These defective motors still grab the filament but do not push it, so they hold the shuttle back. In the laboratory it is hard to control how many motors are active and how many are defective. That makes it hard to test, step by step, how much damage a shuttle can tolerate before it stops moving.

## What we did

We used a mathematical model in place of the experiment. In a model, the fraction of active and defective motors can be set exactly. We varied that fraction and examined how well the shuttles kept moving against the drag of the defective motors, for both kinesin and myosin systems.

## What we found

- The fraction of active motors on the surface is the main factor that decides whether shuttle motion survives.
- For kinesin, about 40% of the motors must be active for shuttles to glide continuously.
- For myosin, about 80% must be active.
- A simple model was enough to describe the motility behaviour.

## Why it matters

A biosensor designer cannot always prevent motor damage during surface preparation. These thresholds give a target: how many motors must survive for the device to work. They also show that a kinesin-based device can tolerate a much larger share of damaged motors than a myosin-based one. Because the model is simple, it helps explain why shuttles keep moving or stop.

## Limits

This summary is written from the published abstract. The thresholds come from a model, not from direct measurement, and they hold for the conditions the model assumes. The abstract does not give the speed of the shuttles at each fraction, or how surface type changes the share of motors that become defective.
