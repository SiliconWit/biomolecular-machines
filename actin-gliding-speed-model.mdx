A biosensor driven by myosin motors relies on actin filaments that glide over the motors and carry the analyte. Speed is the reason to use them: the faster the shuttles move, the sooner the sensor gives a result. But when the motors are fixed to the sensor surface, some become defective. A defective motor binds to the actin filament, stays stuck, and resists the forward push of the active motors. The more defective motors there are, the slower the shuttles glide, and at some point they stop.

A designer needs to know how the gliding speed depends on the share of defective motors. Measuring that in the laboratory is difficult, because the share of defective motors is hard to control and results are hard to reproduce.

## What we did

As the title states, we built a mathematical model that predicts the gliding speed of actin molecular shuttles over myosin motors when defective motors are present.

The related thesis describes the approach used in this line of work. In the 1D analytical model there, a defective motor is assumed to bind to the actin filament, stay bound and stretch as the filament moves, then snap off when a threshold detachment force is reached.

## What we found

No abstract of this chapter is available, so its specific results are not summarised here. The related thesis reports that its 1D analytical model showed actin movement starting when defective motors were reduced to around 20%, and that the range in which actin glides continuously over myosin is small.

## Why it matters

A model that predicts gliding speed from the share of defective motors tells a biosensor designer how much motor damage the device can accept before it becomes too slow. That links the choice of surface material, which decides how many motors stay active, to how the sensor performs.
