Actin filaments pushed by myosin motors move fast, which makes them attractive for fast biosensors. Their weakness is that they are very flexible, so they wander off course. Today, designers keep them on track with microfabricated thin channels. Making those channels adds cost, and a cheaper way to steer the shuttles would allow cheaper devices.

Experiments have shown that an actin filament changes direction when an external force is applied. To use that in a device, a designer needs to know how strong the force should be and in which direction to apply it.

## What we did

We used simulation to study how an external force field steers a gliding actin filament. We applied the field in selected directions and followed how the filament responded, so that the strength and direction of the force could be tuned before an experiment.

## What we found

- The direction of a gliding actin filament can be controlled by selectively applying positive or negative external force fields.
- This control is inexpensive compared with microfabricated channels.

## Why it matters

A biosensor maker who can steer shuttles with an applied field, instead of etched channels, could build devices at lower cost. Knowing in advance how the filament will respond to a given force helps the experimenter choose the field before running the experiment.

## Limits

This summary is written from the published abstract, which is short. The results come from simulation, not experiment. The abstract does not state the force magnitudes tested, how quickly the filament turns, or how reliably it follows the field. It also does not say how the field would be produced in a device.
