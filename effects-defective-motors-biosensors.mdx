A biosensor often waits for the analyte to drift to the detector. Motor proteins such as myosin and kinesin, the motors that move material inside living cells, can do better. They can drive molecular shuttles that carry the analyte actively, which speeds up sensing a great deal.

To work in a biosensor, the motors must be fixed to the sensor surface. That step damages some of them. A defective motor no longer pushes the shuttle, and it slows the detection. A sensor maker needs to know how many damaged motors the device can tolerate, and why.

## What we did

We used a Brownian dynamics simulation to follow molecular shuttles moving over a surface that carries both active and defective motors. By changing the mix of the two, we measured how defective motors affect the transport efficiency of the shuttles, and we looked for the cause.

## What we found

- Shuttle motility depends on the fraction of active motors relative to defective ones.
- For efficient transport, over 90% of the surface-bound motors must remain active.
- The high fraction needed comes from a difference in binding lifetimes. Active and defective motors stay bound to the shuttles for different lengths of time, and that difference decides how strongly the defective motors hold the shuttles back.

## Why it matters

The 90% figure is a demanding target. It tells a biosensor designer that a surface which damages even a modest share of motors can make the shuttles too slow to help. The result explains how motors accumulate on sensor surfaces and gives a guideline for choosing the polymer material of the surface: choose a material that keeps the motors active when they attach.

## Limits

This summary is written from the published abstract. The results come from simulation, not from experiments with a controlled share of defective motors, which are hard to prepare. The abstract does not report which polymer materials perform best or how the threshold changes with motor type or surface density.
