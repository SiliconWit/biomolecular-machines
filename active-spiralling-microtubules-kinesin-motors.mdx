Engineers who build molecular shuttles, tiny transport systems in which surface-bound motor proteins push filaments, need models that predict how the filaments will move. Filaments pushed this way are active polymers, a kind of active matter. When their motion is constrained, they form patterns that a filament at rest never would. A microtubule or actin filament pinned at its leading end, for example, curls into a rotating spiral. The shape of that spiral is set by the balance between the motor forces and the stiffness of the filament.

Earlier theory and simulations modelled the motors implicitly, without mechanics of their own. If the motors themselves deform, those models could predict the wrong spiral.

## What we did

We simulated microtubules pushed by kinesin motors in an overdamped dynamics framework, in which friction dominates and inertia can be neglected. Beyond the mechanics of the spiralling microtubule itself, the model explicitly includes the mechanics of each kinesin motor. We pinned the leading end of the microtubule and followed how and when a spiral formed, across a range of conditions, including the density of motors on the surface.

## What we found

- Spiral formation starts with localized buckling of the microtubule near its pinned end.
- The conditions under which spirals form are summarized in a phase diagram.
- The radius of the spirals scales with the surface motor density with an exponent of about minus 1/4. More motors give tighter spirals.
- This exponent differs from that found in earlier theoretical and simulation studies, which modelled the motors implicitly.
- The difference can be understood as the contribution of the kinesin motors to the total elastic deformation energy of the system.

## Why it matters

The mechanics of the motors are not a detail that a model can leave out. They change how the active polymer behaves, including a measurable quantity such as the spiral radius. For researchers modelling active polymers, this means the motors should be represented explicitly. For designers of devices built on active polymers, such as molecular shuttles driven by motor proteins, it gives a more accurate basis for predicting filament behaviour on a motor-coated surface.

## Limits

This summary is written from the published abstract. The results come from simulation. The abstract reports the scaling exponent as approximate and does not give the range of motor densities, the phase boundaries in numbers, or a direct comparison with experiments.
